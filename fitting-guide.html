Fitting a Finished Room

The walls are already closed. Here is what each piece actually is, whether it still fits, and how the cables get where they need to go without breaking anything.

The ceiling speakers

your question

“Height × 2, in-ceiling above and slightly ahead of the seat — what is this? Is it wireless or wired?”

Wired. There is no wireless version of this. These are passive speakers — they have no power of their own and no radio. The receiver sends them an amplified signal down ordinary speaker cable, exactly like the other five speakers. Two speakers means two cable runs.

What they are: a round speaker, roughly 16 cm across, that sits inside the ceiling with only its grille showing. Flush with the surface. From below it looks like a downlight. Their job is Atmos — rain, a helicopter, footsteps on the floor above — sound that should come from over your head rather than around you.

Your ~20 cm void: confirmed, it fits

These speakers need about 9–10 cm of depth behind the gypsum (KEF Ci160QR: 91 mm; Polk RC80i: ~99 mm). Twenty centimetres is double that — enough for the speaker and the sealed back-box that protects your soundproofing. Position: the two cutouts go in the flat tray field, 1.1–1.6 m ahead of the sofa toward the TV, spaced to line up with the front left and right speakers — and clear of the cove strips and downlight pairs. The exact marks come off the plan once the tape measure confirms the room.

If the ceiling turns out to be solid

Then in-ceiling is out, and you have three honest choices, in the order I would rank them:

  • Upfiring modules — small angled speakers that sit on top of your front pair and bounce sound off the ceiling. Needs a flat, hard ceiling around 2.4–3 m. Not as good as in-ceiling, but real.
  • On-wall height speakers — mounted high on the front wall, angled down at the seat. Cable runs down the wall in trunking. Better than upfiring, more visible.
  • Skip the heights and do a proper 5.1 — and put the money into a better centre speaker or a second subwoofer. A 5.1 done well beats a 5.1.2 done badly, and this is not a compromise you would sit there regretting.

Conduit, and what replaces it

your question

“What is the pull conduit?”

Conduit is just an empty plastic pipe buried in a wall before it is plastered. No cable in it — only a thin nylon string. Later, you tie a cable to the string, pull it through, and you have wiring inside a solid wall without ever breaking it open. When the cable becomes obsolete in eight years, you pull the new one through the same pipe.

Your walls are closed, so that ship has sailed — and it does not matter much, because the finished-room equivalent does the same job. Surface trunking is a slim PVC channel with a snap-off lid that sticks to the wall along the skirting line or the ceiling line. You paint it the colour of the wall and it disappears into the room. Cable goes in, lid goes on, and you can open it any time.

The one thing I would still open a wall for

Nothing here. Every cable in this project can be run without touching plaster, as long as the ceiling is hollow. The build sheet said “before the walls close” because that is the cheap moment — but none of it is impossible afterwards, only slightly less tidy. Do not let anyone tell you the room needs opening up.

Two things this drawing is doing that are worth knowing. The surrounds sit slightly behind your ears, not level with them — that is what makes things pass behind you convincingly. Network and the sofa cable

your questions

“I'll leave the main router in that room, so wireless is an option. I have 500 megabit — is it enough?”

500 Mbps is far more than enough. A 4K stream is around 25 Mbps; a big game download is the only thing that will ever touch the ceiling of that line, and it will finish twenty times faster than you notice. Speed is not your constraint.

Putting the router in the room is the right call, and for a reason you may not have considered: a soundproofed wall is also a Wi-Fi wall. Dense insulation and extra board layers absorb 2.4 and 5 GHz badly. Signal from elsewhere in the house would have arrived weak. Router inside the room solves that — but you should then expect the rooms next to it to get worse coverage, so plan a mesh node outside the door.

Two exceptions where you should still use a cable, and both are trivial because the router is now in the same room: run a short Cat6 to the PC and another to the PS5. Three metres each. Wi-Fi is fine for bandwidth but it jitters, and jitter is what you feel in online play. Two cheap cables remove the whole category of problem.

For the fifth-floor to second-floor run: Cat6 handles 100 m, and three floors is maybe 20–25 m of actual cable. Well inside the limit, no boosters, no fibre needed. Use solid-core Cat6 for the fixed run and terminate it into a wall socket at each end rather than crimping plugs onto solid core, which is fiddly and fails intermittently.

The whole-building plan, one cable

Floor 5: the fiber modem stays, switched to bridge mode, with the Cat6 drop leaving it. Floor 2: the drop feeds your router, then an 8-port gigabit switch (~80 SAR) — wired lines to the TV, the Shield, the PC, the PS5, and the receiver. The family: a mesh kit; your hallway node plugs into the switch (wired backhaul, full speed), and the floor 3–5 nodes mesh up the stairwell. The node serving your floor goes outside the soundproofed door — those walls block Wi-Fi as well as they block sound.

Two rules for the drop itself: outdoor-rated, UV-resistant Cat6 only if any part runs outside the building — the sun destroys normal jackets in a year or two; give it a drip loop before it enters. And never through a cracked-open window: a window that can't seal is a permanent hole in the soundproofing and the AC bill. Drill a small sleeved penetration beside the frame and seal it with acoustic caulk, or use the building riser if one exists.

Desk mode: the big sound with the TV off

The graphics card has more than one output, so run one short HDMI from the GPU directly to a spare receiver input. Pick “Denon AVR” as the sound device in Windows, keep the picture on the monitors, and the full 5.1.2 plays while the TV stays dark — the receiver's Quick Select button makes it one press. This is also the highest-quality PC audio path there is: the receiver gets the raw multichannel signal with nothing in between. For music alone, skip the PC entirely — Spotify Connect, AirPlay, and Bluetooth land straight in the Denon. And none of this trades quality on the TV path: eARC carries Atmos, TrueHD, and DTS:X bit-perfect, so the receiver hears exactly what the source sent, whichever door it came through.

“What is the USB to sofa?”

A small thing, easily explained. Your PC is at the desk. When you sit on the sofa to play a story game on the big screen, your wireless controller still has to talk to the PC — and its little USB receiver is plugged in over at the desk, five metres away with a sofa and your own body in between. It usually works. Sometimes it stutters.

A USB extender is a pair of small boxes joined by a Cat6 cable: one plugs into the PC, the other sits by the sofa and gives you a USB socket there. The controller's receiver goes in that one, half a metre from your hands.

My honest advice: do not buy this yet. Set the room up, sit on the sofa, and play something. If the controller behaves, you have saved 200 SAR and a cable. If it drops out, buy the extender then — and if you are already running trunking to the TV wall, throw a Cat6 in the same channel now while it is open, so the option is there for free. The same applies to the PS5 now that it lives at the desk: the DualSense makes the identical 5 m hop, and the same extender fixes it too if it ever stutters, since the DualSense also works over USB.

Acoustics, in plain terms

your questions

“They put something in the wall for noise. Bass traps, absorption panels — I don't know what these are.”

Your professionals were right, and I want to be clear about that: for stopping sound leaving the room, what they installed is the real thing. That soft material inside your walls is almost certainly mineral wool — rock wool, the grey-brown fibrous stuff that looks a bit like felted cotton. It is the correct material and it is doing its job.

Here is the part that surprises people. An acoustic panel is made of exactly the same material. Same rock wool, in a wooden frame, wrapped in fabric. The only difference is which side of the wall it sits on.

Inside the wall, it stops sound getting out to your neighbours. On the surface of the wall, it stops sound bouncing back at you. Same material, opposite side, completely different job. Your walls do the first one. Nothing in the room currently does the second one — and once you close a room up tightly, the inside surfaces are all hard board and plaster, which is close to the worst case for how sound behaves in there.

A bass trap is the same idea again, just much thicker and shoved into a corner. Low frequencies are physically long waves, so absorbing them needs depth — a 5 cm panel does nothing to bass. And bass piles up in corners, which is why that is where the thick ones go.

Where the panels go, without guessing

There is a trick for this and it needs one other person and a mirror. Sit in your listening seat. Have someone slide a mirror flat along the side wall. Wherever you can see a speaker reflected in the mirror, that spot is a first reflection point — and that is exactly where a panel goes. Mark it with tape. Repeat for the other wall.

The panel map for your room — specific spots, never whole walls

This is the question that matters: you do not cover walls. You catch bounces. Each panel exists to absorb one specific reflection on its way from a speaker to your ears. Once those spots are covered — roughly 20–25% of the wall area — more panels stop helping and start making the room feel unnaturally dead. Most of your black paint stays visible; the panels read as a few deliberate fabric rectangles.

  • Height rule: panel centres at seated ear height — mount them so the panel spans roughly 0.6 to 1.8 m off the floor. Not near the ceiling, not near the skirting.
  • Order of importance: the three behind the sofa first, then the two TV-wall corner traps, then the rest. If the budget squeezes, that first group is 70% of the result.
  • The exact spots get fine-tuned with the mirror trick above once the sofa is in — the map shows the zones; the mirror finds the centimetres.
  • What to ask the supplier for: “8 fabric-wrapped rockwool panels, 120×60×10 cm, black fabric, plus 3 floor-to-ceiling corner bass traps” — that sentence is the whole quote request for acoustic.sa and SNS Arabia.

“The rug — is it a specific rug I have to get?”

No. There is no such thing as an acoustic rug worth paying extra for, whatever the label says. You need a normal rug that meets four conditions:

  • Big enough to cover the floor between the speakers and the sofa — roughly 200 × 300 cm for your room. This is the one that matters most; a small rug in a big floor does very little.
  • Wool rather than synthetic, if the choice is there. It absorbs noticeably better.
  • Medium pile with a dense weave. A thin flat-weave kilim looks wonderful and does almost nothing acoustically.
  • A felt underlay beneath it. Cheap, invisible, and it roughly doubles what the rug is doing. Most people skip this and it is the easiest win in the room.

The floor is the largest hard surface in the room and it sits directly between the speakers and your ears. It is the single most effective thing you will treat, and unlike panels, nobody will ever know it was an acoustic decision.
